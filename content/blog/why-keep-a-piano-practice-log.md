Ask a music teacher what one thing would most improve their students' practice and a lot of them will say: write it down. Ask the students whether they keep a practice journal and almost all of them will say: I tried. There was a notebook. It lasted eleven days.

Both are right. A practice log is the highest-leverage habit available to anyone learning an instrument, and the way most of us try to keep one is designed to fail. This is about what a log is for and how to make one that survives.

## What a log is actually for

Not accountability, or not mainly. A log does three things you cannot do without it.

**It tells you the truth about where the time goes.** Practice is invisible from the inside. You remember the good day and forget the four you skipped. You believe you spend an hour on the Chopin and it is twenty minutes, because the Chopin is hard and the Bach is fun. Everyone who starts logging discovers within a fortnight that their picture of their own practice was wrong, usually in the direction of flattering.

**It carries the thread between sessions.** The first five minutes of most sessions are spent rediscovering where you were: which bar was rough, what tempo you had reached, what you meant to try next. A single line written at the end of yesterday's session — *bars 9–12 clean at 76, join into 13 still rough, try dotted rhythms* — gives those five minutes back, every day. Over a year that is thirty hours.

**It makes progress visible on a timescale you can feel.** Progress at the piano is too slow to notice from the inside. You cannot feel yourself getting better week to week; you can only see it in a record. The tempo that climbed from 60 to 108 over six weeks, the section count going from two to five, the month with twenty-two practiced days — those are the things that keep an adult learner at the instrument through the long flat stretches. Without a record, the flat stretches feel like the whole story.

## What to write

Less than you think. The logs that survive are terse.

At the start of a session: **what you are working on**. One line. The piece, the passage, the tempo you are aiming for. This is the entry that makes the session a session rather than a wander.

At the end: **one line per goal**. What tempo you reached. Which bar is still unreliable. What to try next. Not a diary; a handover note to tomorrow's self, who is a slightly different person with no memory of today.

Occasionally: **a recording**. Nothing in a log is as honest as thirty seconds of audio. The written note says "clean at 76"; the recording says whether it was.

That is all. The elaborate templates — mood, warm-up, technique, repertoire, sight reading, theory, reflection — are why notebooks die on day eleven. Every field you add is a reason not to fill it in.

## Why paper logs die

A paper log asks you to do the recording *after* the thing you wanted to record, when you are tired and the piano is calling you back or the kettle is. It asks you to write the date, the time, the duration, the pieces, all of which you already know, before you get to the one line that matters. And it never adds anything up. Six weeks of entries in a notebook is six weeks of entries; it is not a chart, not a total, not a pattern. The reward for keeping it is the keeping.

The failure is not discipline. It is design. The log is asking for work that a machine should do.

## A log that fills itself in

The fix is to attach the record to something you were going to do anyway: **pressing play**. Chopinly's logbook is built entirely around that. You press play, you say what you are working on, and from that moment every minute belongs to that piece or technique. Switch to scales mid-sitting and the clock follows you; the minutes on the piece stay on the piece. Press stop and the day's list, the calendar and the analytics are already written. Forgot to press play? Hold the button and add the minutes.

The only thing the log asks you to type is the one line that matters — the note. And it puts that note back in front of you the next time you press play on the same piece, so the handover happens whether you remember to look or not.

What you get for it, without further effort:

- **Today**: what you practised, with minutes per goal.
- **History**: a calendar coloured by how long you played each day, a report for any day, and the streak.
- **Each goal's page**: lifetime minutes, every practiced day, the notes thread, the tempo line from the metronome, the takes from the recorder.
- **Analytics**: time by composer, work and type; when in the day you practise; which weekdays carry the load; how long a session tends to run. Tap one composer and every chart recomputes for them.

## What the analytics actually tell you

After a month, the numbers start saying things. Some of the things people find:

- The piece they are "working on" gets a third of the time they believed; the piece they already play well gets the rest.
- Scales never happen on Fridays.
- Sessions after nine at night average twelve minutes. Sessions before work average thirty-five.
- The month they thought was a bad month had nineteen practiced days; the month they thought was good had fourteen with two long Sundays.

None of this is a judgment. It is the truth about where the time went, which is the only basis on which you can decide where it should go.

## Keeping it going

The habit is the play button, not the log. If you press play every time you sit down, the log keeps itself. The notes are the one thing that needs you, and the rule for those is the same as for everything else in practice: make the bar low. One line. If you write "ok" and nothing else, the day still counts, and the tempo the metronome stamped is still on the chart.

Then, once a month, read back. Look at the calendar. Look at the tempo line. Play this month's take against last month's. That is the moment the log pays for itself — the moment you see, in evidence rather than feeling, that the slow days added up to something.
