Everyone who plays the piano has asked this question, and most of the answers are either a number pulled from a conservatory brochure or a shrug. Here is a more useful answer: it depends on what you want, but the amount is almost always **smaller than people think, and the frequency almost always higher**. Let me unpack that.

## The honest ranges

For an adult beginner or a returning player who wants to enjoy the instrument and make visible progress, **twenty to thirty minutes a day, five or six days a week** is enough to learn real pieces and keep improving for years. For a serious amateur working towards a recital or an exam, **forty-five to ninety minutes** most days. For a student aiming at a conservatory, several hours, structured — and that is a different article and a different life.

The important word in every one of those sentences is *day*. Ten minutes a day for a week is better than seventy minutes on Sunday. It is not slightly better. It is a different activity.

## Why frequency wins

Motor learning happens between sessions as much as within them. Sleep consolidates what you practised; the passage that was shaky at the end of Tuesday's session is often cleaner on Wednesday morning without a note played in between. Six sessions give you six consolidation cycles. One long session gives you one, plus a lot of tired repetitions at the end that consolidate the tiredness.

There is also the plain matter of attention. Practice that changes anything requires attention, and attention lasts somewhere between fifteen and forty minutes before it degrades. Past that point you are still at the piano, but you are playing through, not practising. A ninety-minute session done badly is three twenty-minute sessions of good work and thirty minutes of habit-forming noise.

## Why "focused" is not a platitude

The distinction between practising and playing is the whole thing. Twenty minutes with a specific goal, a small passage, a slow tempo and a test at the end will change the passage. An hour of playing through the pieces you already know will not change anything except your mood. Both are fine; only one is practice. When someone asks how long to practise, they usually mean the first, and when they describe what they did, it is usually the second. So the first step in deciding how long to practise is to decide what counts.

A practical rule: a session counts as practice if you can name what was different at the end. If you cannot, it was playing, which is allowed and is not the answer to this question.

## Set the amount you will actually do

The second most common failure is aiming for an hour, managing it twice, missing a day, feeling that the streak is broken, and stopping for a month. The amount you should practise is the amount you will still be practising in six months. For most adults with jobs that is twenty minutes, and the right response to "only twenty minutes?" is to look at the calendar in six months.

Set the bar so low it is embarrassing. Ten minutes. Then the rule is not "practise for ten minutes" but "sit down and press play", because once you are at the piano with the clock running, ten minutes usually becomes twenty-five on its own. The hard part was never the practising; it was the sitting down.

Chopinly's history calendar colours each day by how long you played. The point of the colour is not to shame the short days. It is to make the short days visible as *days*, because a pale dot is a session and a blank is not, and a month of pale dots is a habit.

## Breaking a long session up

If you do have an hour, do not spend it as an hour. Two thirty-minute blocks with a break between them — a walk, a coffee, an errand — will out-perform the unbroken hour, and three twenties will out-perform the two thirties. The break is where the passage settles. Coming back to it cold after fifteen minutes is also a free test: if it is still there, it was learned.

Within a block, split the time across goals rather than giving the whole block to one. Fifteen minutes on the hard passage, ten on the new section, five on scales. The switch of material refreshes attention, and Chopinly's clock lets you change goals mid-sitting without losing the minutes already logged on the first one.

## How to know it is enough

You do not know by counting minutes. You know by looking at what changed. Every couple of weeks, ask:

- Did the tempo on the hard piece climb? (The metronome stamps a line on the goal's page; it should slope up.)
- Is there a new section that was not there before?
- Does the recording from this week sound different from the recording from a month ago?

If the answer is yes to two of the three, the amount is enough. If it is no to all three, the problem is probably not the amount; it is what the minutes were spent on. Look at the analytics by goal and see whether the time actually went where you meant it to. A common discovery is that the piece you think you are working on gets a third of the minutes you thought, and the piece you already play well gets the rest, because it is more fun.

## When to practise

Whenever you will. That said, there are two useful facts. Early in the day, before the decisions of work have used up your attention, sessions tend to be more focused. And a short session late in the evening, right before sleep, seems to consolidate unusually well — ten minutes on the passage you are trying to memorise, then bed, is an old musicians' trick that the sleep research has caught up with.

Chopinly's analytics show when in the day you actually practise, and how long a session tends to run at each hour. Most people find their evening sessions are shorter than they thought and their weekend sessions longer, and adjust accordingly.

## The summary you can put on the piano

- Twenty to thirty minutes, most days, with a goal. That is enough to learn real music for the rest of your life.
- Six short days beat one long one. Sleep is part of practice.
- If you have an hour, split it. If you have ten minutes, sit down anyway.
- Judge the amount by what changed, not by the clock.
- The hard part is sitting down. Make the bar low enough that you always clear it.
