Sight singing for pianists: the ten-minute habit that fixes your sight reading

September 6, 2026 · 5 min read · by Leif Taylor

Pianists avoid singing, and their sight reading suffers for it. Why singing a line before you play it is the fastest route to inner hearing, how to start when you don't think you can sing, and a routine that fits before breakfast.

Nobody takes up the piano because they wanted to sing. Many of us took it up partly to avoid singing. So it is inconvenient that the single most effective thing a pianist can do for their sight reading, their memory and their musicianship is to sing — badly, quietly, alone in a room — for ten minutes a day.

This is about why that is true and how to do it without either a choir or a voice.

The problem sight singing solves

Sight reading at the piano is usually taught as a decoding skill: see the note, find the key, press it, fast. It works, up to a point. The point is the one where the notes are coming faster than decoding can go, and at that point every sight reader falls back on something else — an expectation of what comes next, built from hearing the line in their head half a beat ahead of their hands. Readers who have that expectation read fluently. Readers who do not stumble at every leap.

That expectation is called inner hearing, or audiation, and there is one reliable way to build it: read a line and produce it without the instrument doing the pitching for you. Which means singing. When you sing a written line, you cannot fake it. Either you heard the interval before you sang it, or you sang the wrong note, and the tuner or your ear tells you which. A hundred short lines later, the intervals on the page have sounds attached to them, and the next time you sight read at the piano the sound arrives before the hand does.

"But I can't sing"

You can. Not well, not in tune yet, not for an audience — none of that is required. Sight singing is not singing in the performance sense. It is pitching, out loud, so that your ear can check your reading. A thin, breathy, wobbly note on the right pitch is a full success. A beautiful note on the wrong one is a miss.

Two things make it easier than you expect. First, you are allowed to sing in whatever octave your voice lives in; a baritone singing a soprano line an octave down is singing it correctly. Second, you are allowed to be quiet. Most of the practice in this article can be done at a hum.

Starting from the piano

The pianist's advantage is that the reference is always in reach. Use it, then use it less.

Week one. Sound the tonic on the piano (or the pitch pipe). Sing the scale up and down on a neutral syllable — la, or numbers. Then take a short melody in that key, stepwise only, and sing it, checking each note against the piano after you sing it, not before. Notice which steps you undershoot; most people sing rising seconds flat and falling seconds sharp.

Week two. Same, with thirds. Sing the scale in thirds (1–3, 2–4, 3–5 …), then melodies that leap by a third. Check less often — every phrase rather than every note.

Week three. Fourths and fifths, the tonic triad as an arpeggio (1–3–5–3–1), melodies built from it. Now sing the whole line, then check only the last note against the piano. If it matches, every interval on the way was correct.

After that. Wider leaps, minor keys, accidentals, and rhythms that are not just quarters. Keep the same rule: sing, then check.

Rhythm is half of it

The other thing sight singing does that playing cannot is expose your rhythm. At the piano, a wrong rhythm still produces the right notes and it is easy not to notice. Sung, a wrong rhythm is obvious because the tune comes out wrong. So sing with a pulse — tap it, or run the metronome quietly — and treat a rhythm miss as seriously as a pitch miss.

A good check when a line goes wrong: clap the rhythm alone, then sing the pitches alone without rhythm, then put them back together. Whichever half fails alone is the half to drill. Chopinly's judge scores the two separately for exactly this reason.

Solfège, numbers, or la?

Any of them, and the argument about which is the best is mostly a way of avoiding practice. Numbers (1–7) have the advantage for pianists that they map directly onto scale degrees, which is how you will think about the keyboard anyway. Movable-do solfège is the same idea with syllables that are easier to sing. Neutral la is fine to begin with and stops helping once you want to name what you hear. Pick one, use it for a month, and then decide.

A routine that fits before breakfast

  • Two minutes: tonic, scale, arpeggio. Check with the piano or the pitch pipe.
  • Six minutes: three or four short exercises at your current level, sung once each, checked at the end.
  • Two minutes: one exercise from the level above. Expect to miss. That is where the ear is being built.

Ten minutes, at the piano or away from it. The log will show a small entry every day, folded from the individual runs, and after a month the scores on each book will have moved.

What changes at the piano

The payoff shows up somewhere you were not looking for it. A month or two in, you will open a new piece and find that you are reading the melody before you play it — a bar ahead, sometimes two — and that the leaps that used to stop you are landing because you heard where they were going. Memory improves for the same reason: a line you can sing is a line you know, independent of the fingering. And the wrong notes you play in a fast passage start to sting immediately, rather than three bars later.

None of that requires a voice. It requires ten minutes and the willingness to hum, off-key, at a page of notes, until the notes start to hum back.
