Stuck on a plateau? What to change when piano practice stops working

September 6, 2026 · 7 min read · by Leif Taylor

Every pianist hits a stretch where the hours go in and nothing comes out. Plateaus are usually a sign that the practice has stopped being practice. How to diagnose which kind you are on, and the specific changes that get things moving again.

The first year at the piano is a slope: every week something new is possible. Then, without warning, the slope flattens. You practise as much as before, or more, and the pieces do not get cleaner, the tempo does not climb, and the new piece takes as long as the last one did. It lasts weeks, or months. Some people quit here, quietly, telling themselves they reached their ceiling.

There is no ceiling at the level where most plateaus happen. What there is, almost always, is practice that has stopped doing anything — repetition that has become rehearsal, goals that have become locations, sessions that have become a habit of sitting down. The good news is that this is diagnosable from the outside, and the diagnosis tells you what to change.

First, check whether you are actually on one

Plateaus are partly perception. Progress at the piano is slow and continuous and you cannot feel continuous things; you can only feel steps, and steps come at irregular intervals. So the first thing to do is look at the evidence rather than the feeling.

If you keep a practice log, look at the last eight weeks. Did the tempo on the hard piece climb — even by ten beats? Is there a section that exists now that did not exist two months ago? Play this week's recording against one from two months back. Chopinly puts all of this on the goal's page: the tempo sparkline, the practiced days, the takes, the notes thread. Often the answer is that things moved and you did not notice, because you were standing on them.

If the evidence agrees with the feeling — the line is flat, the takes sound the same — read on.

Plateau one: the practice became playing

The most common plateau, by far. Somewhere along the way the sessions turned into playing through the pieces you know, with a few minutes on the hard bit at the end. It is pleasant, it feels like practice, and it changes nothing.

How to tell: look at the analytics by goal. If the piece you are "working on" gets less time than the pieces you already play, or if the sessions are long but the notes on each goal are empty, this is it. Another sign: you cannot say, at the end of a session, what is different.

The fix: for two weeks, no playing through until the last five minutes. Every session starts with one small unit — two to four bars — a metronome tempo where you can play it three times clean, and the tempo stamped on the goal each time you raise it. There is a longer piece on this: how to practice effectively. The plateau usually breaks in the first week, and it is a little humiliating how quickly.

Plateau two: the tempo wall

The piece is clean at 100, and at 108 it falls apart, and it has been that way for a month. Pushing harder at 108 makes it worse.

How to tell: the tempo line on the goal's page is flat at one number, with attempts above it that did not stick.

The fix: a tempo wall is almost never a speed problem. It is a technique problem that the slow tempo was hiding — a fingering that works at 100 and not at 108, a hand position change that has too far to travel, a thumb that is late. Go back to 80. Find the exact beat where it breaks at 108 by playing 108 and stopping at the first stumble. Then look at what the hand is doing on that beat, at 80, in slow motion. Change the fingering, redistribute a note to the other hand, prepare the position a beat early. Then climb again from 80, and the wall is usually gone by the time you get back to 108, because the thing that built it is not there.

If that fails: rhythms. Dotted, reverse-dotted, in groups of three against the four. Each rhythm forces a different set of notes to be fast, and the hand learns the whole passage at speed without ever playing it at speed.

Plateau three: the ear is behind the hands

This one is subtle and it shows up around the second or third year. Pieces get learned, but slowly, and the learning is all fingering — there is no sense of where the music goes, memory is fragile, sight reading is stuck, and playing by ear is impossible. The hands have got ahead of the ear, and the ear is now the limit.

How to tell: can you sing the melody of the passage you are working on? Can you hear it in your head, away from the piano? If not, this is it.

The fix: ten minutes a day of ear training and sight singing, for a month, at the piano. It will feel like a detour from the pieces. It is not; it is the thing the pieces are waiting for. Players who do this report the next piece going in about twice as fast, and staying in.

Plateau four: too much repertoire, too little depth

Six pieces in progress, none finished, each getting eight minutes a session. Everything moves a little; nothing gets done.

How to tell: the analytics by work show the time spread thin, and the log shows no piece has had a concentrated week in months.

The fix: pick one. Finish it — really finish it, memorized, recorded, played for someone. Then the next. Depth on one piece teaches things that breadth on six never does, and the feeling of finishing is what a plateau most lacks.

Plateau five: the body

Tension. A rising shoulder, a locked wrist, a thumb that grips. It shows up as a plateau because tense playing has a speed limit and a fatigue limit, and both are low. It also shows up as pain, and pain is where this stops being an article and starts being a conversation with a teacher.

How to tell: record yourself with the camera, not just the microphone. Watch the shoulders. Notice whether you can play the hard passage for five minutes without the forearm burning.

The fix: slower, softer, and with attention on the release of each note rather than the attack. Hanon-type exercises done tense make this worse; done with a teacher watching the wrist, they can help. If there is pain, stop, and get a teacher who works on technique in the room with you. No app fixes this one.

Plateau six: nothing is wrong, you are tired

Sometimes the practice is fine and the player is not. Work, sleep, a hard month. Everything takes longer because everything takes longer.

How to tell: the log shows the sessions got shorter and later without you deciding that. The analytics say sessions after 9 pm average fourteen minutes.

The fix: shrink the target, not the habit. Ten minutes, early, on one passage, and nothing else. Keep the streak of days alive at that level until the month passes. Coming back from a gap is much harder than coasting through one.

The general principle

Every plateau is a sign that whatever you are doing has stopped producing information. The passage that is played the same way every day teaches nothing new; the session with no goal cannot fail and so cannot succeed. The way off is always the same: change something so that the practice can tell you something — a smaller unit, a lower tempo, a different rhythm, a different skill, a recording, a different hour. Then look at the evidence a week later.

Which is the real argument for keeping a log at all. A plateau you can see is a problem you can solve. A plateau you can only feel is just a bad month that goes on until you quit.
