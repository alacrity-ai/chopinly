Ear training for pianists: why the keys let you skip it, and how to stop skipping it

September 6, 2026 · 7 min read · by Leif Taylor

Pianists can play music they cannot hear. That is the instrument's gift and its trap. A practical route into ear training at the piano: what to drill, in what order, and how ten minutes a day at the instrument rewires the ear to the hands.

In this article

  3. The order that works

A violinist with a bad ear plays out of tune and everybody knows within a bar. A pianist with a bad ear plays perfectly in tune, because the piano tuner has done the hearing for them, and can get remarkably far before anyone notices — sometimes as far as a conservatory audition. The piano lets you read a map (the score) onto another map (the keyboard) with the ears switched off, and many of us learned to do exactly that.

Then one day you have to play something you have only heard. Or transpose a hymn down a tone while the congregation waits. Or catch your own wrong note in a fast passage before the next bar, rather than three bars later when the harmony has gone strange. Or sing a line to a student. The maps run out and there is nothing underneath.

Ear training is the thing underneath. Here is how to build it, at the piano, without turning it into a second instrument to feel guilty about.

What "a good ear" actually means

It is not perfect pitch. Perfect pitch — knowing a lone note's name with no reference — is rare, mostly fixed in early childhood, and not what musicians use most of the time. What working musicians rely on is relative pitch: given one note, knowing what every other note is in relation to it. Hear the tonic, hear a melody, know that it went up a fifth and down a third. Hear a chord, know it is a minor triad in first inversion. Relative pitch is learnable by anyone at any age, and pianists are unusually well placed to learn it because the reference is always under the hand.

Concretely, an ear that is "good enough" for a pianist can:

  • Sing back a short melody after hearing it once, in the right key.
  • Hear a note against a known tonic and name its scale degree.
  • Hear two or three notes at once and pick them out.
  • Notice, while playing, that the note that just sounded was not the one written.
  • Play a simple tune by ear in a couple of tries.

None of those requires a gift. They require a few hundred small repetitions, each with feedback.

Why drilling at the piano is different

Most ear-training apps live on a screen and expect you to answer on a screen. That trains the ear, but it trains it in isolation from the hands, and the whole point for a pianist is the connection: hearing a note and having the hand go there. When you answer an ear-training question by playing the note on your actual piano, three things happen at once. The ear identifies the pitch, the hand finds it, and — this is the part that matters — the feeling of the hand finding it gets bound to the sound. After a few weeks the binding runs in both directions: you hear a fifth and the hand knows where it is; you see a leap of a fifth in the score and you hear it before you play it.

Chopinly's ear training was built around this. The drill plays a reference and then a target, and you can answer on the on-screen keyboard or turn on the microphone and answer on your real piano. The app rounds what it hears to the nearest semitone, with plenty of tolerance for a piano that isn't perfectly in tune, and judges the pitch class, so the right note in any octave counts. It mutes the mic during its own sounds so it never hears itself.

The order that works

There is a lot of ear training material, and most of it starts in the wrong place — with interval identification out of context, which is hard and oddly useless. Start with a key.

1. Scale degrees in one key, one octave. Hear the tonic, hear a note, play it. Everything is in C major or whatever key the app is set to, one octave, single notes, a reference before each question. This is embarrassingly easy for about a week and then it is not, because the app starts asking for 6 and 7 and you find you cannot tell 6 from 4 without singing up the scale. That singing is the work. Do it out loud. Chopinly's beginner preset is exactly this drill.

2. Two notes at once, same key. A dyad sounds; play both notes. The ear has to separate them, which is a new skill — most people hear the top note easily and have to hunt for the bottom one. This is the beginning of hearing harmony. The intermediate preset.

3. Wider range, then chromatic notes, then three at a time. Once the diatonic notes are automatic, widen the range to two octaves so the same scale degree in a different register does not fool you. Then allow every pitch — the chromatic notes are the ones that catch you in real music. Then triads. The advanced preset covers this ground with a single reference at the start of the run rather than before every question, which is how real music works: you get the key once and hold it.

4. Sing before you play. At any stage, add one rule: before you play the answer, sing it. If you can sing it, you have heard it; if you cannot, you are guessing at the keyboard and the drill is teaching your hands to guess.

Ten minutes a day is enough. Chopinly folds a day's drills into one entry in your practice log so a dozen two-minute runs do not clutter the history, and each run is scored so you can watch the number climb.

What a miss should teach you

The most valuable moment in ear training is the wrong answer, provided you find out how it was wrong. "Wrong" teaches nothing; "you played a fourth and it was a fifth" teaches everything, because fourths-for-fifths is a specific, fixable confusion (the two intervals are inversions of each other, and the ear hears the shape before the size). Chopinly's drill names the miss — the right note in the wrong octave, a sixth instead of a third — and after a week the misses cluster. Whatever clusters is your next drill.

Common clusters, and the fix:

  • 4 vs 5: sing the tonic, then sing up to the note. A fourth stops at fa; a fifth goes on to sol.
  • 6 vs 3: same trick, from the top down. Both are "a third-ish shape", but 6 sits above 5.
  • Right note, wrong octave: not a hearing error, a register habit. Turn the range down to one octave for a week, then back up.
  • Anything chromatic: name it as a neighbour. The note between 4 and 5 is either sharp-4 (leading up) or flat-5 (leaning down); hear which way it wants to go.

Carrying it into repertoire

The drill is scaffolding; the piece is the building. Three habits bring the ear into the practice room:

  • Sing the melody of the passage before you play it. If you cannot, you do not know the passage; you know the fingering.
  • Name the bass. Before a phrase, say the scale degrees of the bass line. Then play and listen for them.
  • Play by ear for five minutes. Any tune — a song from the radio, the melody you just sang. Wrong notes are fine; the hunting is the practice.

Sight singing is the other half of this, and Chopinly has a tool for it, but the ear-training drills are the place to start because they give feedback on a single note at a time, which is where the ear is built.

How long it takes

Longer than a week and shorter than you fear. Most adults who do ten focused minutes a day at the piano find scale degrees in one key automatic within a month, dyads within two, and can pick out a simple melody by ear within three. The advanced drills stay hard for a long time, which is fine; that is what advanced means.

The change that matters comes sooner than any of those milestones. Somewhere in the first few weeks you will play a wrong note in a piece and hear it, immediately, before your eyes get back to the score. That is the ear switching on. Everything after that is refinement.
